# Case: how Papermark tested demand

Source: "How to Clone Software and Make $3m Fast", Top Founders with Nathan Latka, 10 Nov 2026, with Marc Seitz, co-founder of Papermark. These figures are the founder's claims in the interview and were not independently verified.

## What they did, step by step

1. **Picked an incumbent everyone knew and nobody loved.** DocSend was acquired by Dropbox during the Covid bubble and "kind of stopped iterating on the product". The market already understood the category, so "most of the time our customers use already a comparable tool… they don't need any education."
2. **Tested with one post before building.** The rule came from his co-founder and wife: "don't just build something… test it on Twitter first. If people like it, if they share it, comment… then you can build it over the weekend." The tweet pitched an open-source DocSend alternative and got 450 likes and 95,000 views. He had about 1,000 followers at the time.
3. **Shipped version 1 in a weekend.** It went viral a second time on launch.
4. **Used a second signal: stars.** The first ~1,000 GitHub stars came within 1–2 months, from the viral posts plus an existing open-source community. "Once I hit 1,000 stars, I was validated."
5. **Got the strongest signal last: people asking to pay.** "the first customers started saying, hey, can we pay you money?"
6. **Exploited a gap the incumbent opened.** "DocSend eventually killed their free version. That's an opening for us." Papermark keeps a free plan, gets about 100 signups a day and about 100 new paying customers a month.
7. **Differentiated without changing user behaviour.** They added custom domains and full branding, whereas the incumbents "put their own brand front and center". Free-plan links default to papermark.com, which creates a viral loop. Papermark has 60,000 teams.
8. **Reached feature parity before growth came.** It took about 2 years to reach $1M ARR (Sep 2025), then 4 months to reach $2M (Jan 2026). "we were missing maybe 1 or 2 features… they still had to go to a competitor." Growth came once the product was complete at a lower price.

## The demand test in one line

A cloneable incumbent (known, disliked, stagnant, recently made worse) + a public "I'll build the free/open alternative" post + pre-agreed thresholds (likes/shares/comments) + a weekend build + escalating signals (stars → signups → "can we pay you").

## What to copy

- **Pre-commit to a rule before posting** ("only build if…"). It stops you rationalising weak results.
- **Test the positioning, not the product:** "open-source/free/cheaper X alternative" is one sentence the market already understands.
- **Look for an incumbent that just got worse:** acquired, stagnant, price hike, killed its free plan.
- **Climb the evidence ladder:** engagement → stars or waitlist → free signups → people asking to pay.
- **Check whether parity is reachable.** If the incumbent's must-have features take years to match, expect a slow start, as Papermark had.

## What not to over-learn (caveats)

- **Survivorship bias.** He had "five, six failures" first and calls Papermark "a lucky break… I don't know if you could replicate this in a lab."
- **The audience wasn't zero.** He had about 1,000 followers and an existing open-source peer group that "had each other's back".
- **Engagement isn't purchase intent.** Likes are the weakest rung. The business was proven by paying customers years later, not by the tweet.
- **The channel shifted.** "None of our customers today are on Twitter… Most of them are on LinkedIn." The validation channel and the buying channel can differ.
- **Timing.** The open-source "alternative" wave was strong in 2023. The same post may land differently in other markets or years.
